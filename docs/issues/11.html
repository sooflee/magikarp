Exponential trends to watch.

Signals to watch

Inference costs reverse toward custom silicon · active regime
After years of collapsing inference prices, the newest frontier models are raising them, pushing companies from general-purpose GPUs toward custom chips. AMD agreed on August 6 to buy Taalas, which burns a model's weights into the chip's own wiring: its HC1 encodes Llama 3.1 8B across 53 billion transistors and is quoted near 17,000 tokens per second per user at about 200 watts.
What to watch: OpenAI unveiled its first inference chip, Jalapeño, co-designed with Broadcom, at Hot Chips on August 25, and SemiAnalysis published a teardown the same day: TSMC's N3P with HBM4, 128 chips per rack scaling to 2,048 in one domain, initial deployment targeted for the end of 2026, and a claimed perf-per-watt lead the piece itself attributes to software co-design rather than silicon. Nvidia said the next day its gross margin will trough at 71 to 72 percent in the fourth quarter on memory costs. Watch whether HBM contracts, not GPU lists, set the next price moves. Where it shows up: AMD, Marvell, Broadcom, and other custom-silicon makers.
Resource-scarcity chains · active
Droughts ripple through several steps before they reach markets, and each step tends to amplify the one before it. NOAA's August 13 update gives a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong El Niño this winter and a 69 percent chance of a historic one exceeding every event since 1950, while the Drought Monitor recorded rapidly deteriorating conditions across Oklahoma and the Texas Panhandle in the week to August 11.
What to watch: Wheat rose 12.6 percent in the week to August 28, its biggest gain since March 2022, on a European heat wave, drought in the Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas belt, and Black Sea disruption after the Novorossiysk strikes; corn added 5.8 percent, soybeans 4.2 and cocoa 9 on a West African crop estimate 18 percent smaller. The August 25 Drought Monitor recorded rapid expansion across Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana and Arkansas. NOAA's next El Niño update is September 10. Where it shows up: Grain prices, natural gas, and water-stressed chipmakers.
AI's off-balance-sheet financing · watch (financing)
The AI buildout is increasingly funded outside company books. On August 10 Nvidia and Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR announced platforms to mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party capital through vehicles that issue debt and lease compute, with Nvidia providing up to 25 percent financing support per deal; Nikkei had already counted about $1.65 trillion of special-purpose-vehicle debt tied to five US tech giants. Each layer of guarantees lets the next project borrow more.
What to watch: New special-purpose-vehicle debt, vendor financing and backstops from chipmakers, and any credit-rating action on AI-heavy issuers. Reports this week put Big Tech's second-quarter 'other income' past $160 billion on paper gains in private AI companies, more than double the roughly $69 billion of the quarter before, with Alphabet alone booking $97.9 billion; the concern is marks on OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX stakes flowing through as profit. SB Energy's IPO filing carries about $5.5 billion of warrants granted to OpenAI. Where it shows up: Oracle, Meta, data-center debt vehicles, and Nvidia.

Replying 'AI;DR' is becoming an acceptable answer to pasted model text.

Undercurrent

Rick Manelius coined 'AI;DR' on August 17 for the reply you give unedited model output: if the sender did not bother to edit it, the reader will not bother to read it. The post reached number one on Hacker News at 1,111 points, and a companion manifesto, dontpastetheai.com, drew 1,044 points for asking people to answer questions in their own words, in the tradition of nohello.net. Both landed the same week Responsible Statecraft caught a government paying $900,000 for exactly the kind of text readers are learning to skip.

  • Don't paste the AI, please
The wider world

China scrubs its moon-water launch on the pad, and lab-grown brain tissue turns out to keep time.steady

Deep-dive of the week

China rolled Chang'e-7 to the pad at Wenchang on August 19 and announced on August 23 that the probe 'does not meet launch conditions' and cannot fly in the planned window this year, after a tropical storm forced a scrub and the narrow trans-lunar window lapsed. The mission is the flagship robotic hunt for water ice near the lunar south pole: an orbiter, lander, rover and a hopping probe aimed at the Shackleton crater region. The same week, Harvard reported cortical organoids grown for more than five years whose cells aged on the body's schedule, and TU Wien began building an electron microscope with a quantum computer inside it.

  • China abruptly postpones ambitious Chang'e-7 lunar mission launch (Phys.org)
  • Human brain organoids record the passage of time over multiple years (Nature)
  • Quantum computer microscope set to improve electron microscopy (Phys.org)
  • JWST finds hidden stars in the universe's most ancient galaxies (Space.com)

Officials told international partners the Chang'e-7 slip is at least a month, with backup windows running from late September through April 2027; SpaceNews read the official 'this year' language as pointing to 2027. US landers are aimed at the same south-pole region on dates that have themselves slipped repeatedly.

Fifty percent tariffs land on Canada as the talks collapse over final terms.elevated

Geopolitics

Trade talks in Washington broke down late on August 21, and 50 percent tariffs on about $20 billion of Canadian goods took effect at midnight, with energy, potash and critical minerals carved out. Carney set dollar-for-dollar retaliation for September 8 and said the next day that 'you're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked.'. In Kyiv, an overnight barrage of missiles and 168 drones on August 19 and 20 killed 17 people, 15 of them in the city itself and most of those in one nine-storey block, while fuel rationing spread across more than half of Russia's regions. New Zealand moved to ban under-16s from social media, Uzbekistan appeared to take delivery of Chinese fighter jets, and Pakistan's Supreme Court fought the government over a jailed former prime minister's heart exam.

US-Canada talks collapse and 50 percent tariffs take effect (Bloomberg)
Talks broke down late on August 21, and Canada's ambassador, Mark Wiseman, said what had been agreed 'was quite different from what was showing up in the documents'. The duties cover about $20 billion of goods including alcohol, dairy and cement, with energy, potash and critical minerals carved out. Trump wrote on August 23 that 'Canada wants the benefits of being a State, without being one'. The trade-fragmentation regime now runs through North America's most integrated border.

Russian missiles and 168 drones kill 17 in Kyiv as air defences run low (Moscow Times)
Fifteen of the 17 died in the city itself, most in one nine-storey block in Solomianskyi; Zelenskyy said on August 20 that Patriot interceptors 'cannot yet be replaced, and we need them every day', then rejected calls for a wartime election as a 'tsunami' that could split the country. On the other side, Izvestia counted fuel available at 28.1 percent of Russian stations on August 16, down from 41 a week earlier, and Putin said Ukraine had opened a 'Pandora's box' and should expect a response aimed at its 'most sensitive economic sectors'. The war is now an energy war in both directions.

New Zealand moves to ban under-16s from social media (Bloomberg)
Luxon's Online Safety Bill, announced August 24 in Wellington, would fine 'high risk' platforms up to 10 percent of global revenue for failing to check ages by ID, facial estimation or account data; both coalition partners oppose it, and Luxon said it will not reach a first reading before Parliament rises for the November election. The checking machinery is the age-assurance infrastructure already on our watchlist.

Uzbekistan appears to take delivery of Chinese J-10CE fighters (Eurasianet)
Eurasianet reports four Chengdu J-10CE fighters delivered under an agreement for a reported 24, which neither government has confirmed, and frames the deal as China filling the arms-supply vacuum left by Russia's war, with cooperation widening in Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan. Central Asia's security guarantees used to run through Moscow.

Pakistan's top court orders Imran Khan to hospital; the government sends him back to jail (Al Jazeera)
The Supreme Court ordered the 73-year-old former prime minister moved to Shifa International within two days, after a jail cardiac board on August 10 recorded fluctuating blood pressure and anxiety tied to restricted family contact. He has been imprisoned since August 2023.

The Treasury starts buying twice as many of its own long bonds, and gold and bitcoin rise while stocks fall.

Markets

The 30-year Treasury yield touched its highest since 2007 early in the week, and on August 19 the Treasury answered: buyback operations in 10- to 30-year debt roughly double, to $4 billion and more per operation, from September 9 through November 4. Ten-year yields fell 6 basis points that day, the dollar ended the week down 0.9 percent, gold closed a third straight weekly gain at a three-month high, and bitcoin rose 24 percent in a squeeze that wiped out a record $2.7 billion of crypto short positions in the 24 hours around August 19, after the SEC proposed a crypto rulebook on August 18 and Trump pressed Congress for the CLARITY Act at a White House event the next day. The S&P 500 fell 1.4 percent, its first weekly loss since late July, on rising long-end yields and the oil-driven inflation scare, though Friday's flash services PMI ran hot at 56.8, a 20-month high. Volatility measures how much the market is expected to move in the near term compared with the longer term, so a lower reading means less immediate stress. The yield curve is the gap between long-term and short-term government borrowing rates, and a steep curve usually points to expected growth rather than recession. When crypto is described as risk-off, investors are stepping back from the most speculative assets, which often serves as an early note of caution beneath a calm market.

Shein prices its listing at a quarter of its 2022 valuation and volunteers for a US security review.

The wildcard · Companies and deals

Shein set September 1 as the target for its Hong Kong debut, seeking up to about $1.8 billion at a valuation near $27 billion, down from $98 billion in its 2022 private round; planned London and New York listings never happened, and UBS Asset Management joined as a cornerstone investor after Reuters reported soft demand. On August 23 Bloomberg reported the company voluntarily asked CFIUS, the US national-security review panel, to examine its $80 million purchase of the American retailer Everlane, a filing companies almost never make after a deal has closed, because clearance would shield its US expansion from a later unwind. The prospectus shows 2025 profit down 38.7 percent to $2.06 billion after the US and EU ended duty-free small parcels, the cost advantage the company was built on.

Instrument hacking (NBER working paper, Keane, Neal and Vu)
Simulations show that trying candidate instruments until one 'works' biases instrumental-variable estimates back toward ordinary least squares, and the bias grows with each extra candidate tried.

FTC backs antitrust scrutiny of patent-application buyouts in biologics (FTC, August 21)
The agency told the Fourth Circuit that buying rights to a rival's pending patent applications is open to antitrust scrutiny, in CareFirst's Sherman Act suit alleging Amgen acquired and shaped applications covering Enbrel to extend its monopoly; the Commission voted 2-0.
